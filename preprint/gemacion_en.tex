\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Conversational agents are increasingly expected to remember what a user told them across sessions,
including the case where the user \emph{corrects} something said earlier. A natural proposal is to
exploit the geometry of the embedding space: rather than overwriting a memory, deposit the revised
version \emph{near} its predecessor, so that proximity encodes the relation between versions. We test
that proposal directly, in a non-parametric index over a frozen encoder, with every prediction
registered and hash-frozen before the corresponding data existed.

The result is a consistent negative with an identified mechanism. With a fixed displacement step, the
revision cluster performs an unbounded random walk away from its anchor: cosine to the query falls
from $0.811$ to $0.324$ after four revisions and to $-0.139$ after six, and coverage collapses to
$0.000$ from $K \geq 4$. Bounding the displacement repairs the drift exactly as intended --- the
cosine becomes flat at $0.8177$, slope $+0.0000$ per revision --- and the mechanism \emph{still}
loses, by $-0.0864$ (95\% CI $[-0.1014, -0.0714]$) at $K=8$. The reason is a constant toll of
$\approx 0.036$ cosine: the embedding of the revision's own text is already optimally placed, because
it contains the entity the query mentions, so any artificial displacement can only move it away.

That last clause is a property of the \emph{generator}, not of corrections. Real conversational
corrections are elliptical: ``no, it's Beto'' does not name the entity. We therefore ran an
adversarial third experiment in the regime that should favour geometry, against the honest baseline
(coreference hydration, as deployed by production memory systems), sweeping its failure rate $\tau$.
Geometry only wins above $\tau^{*} \approx 0.45$ --- a coreference resolver worse than a coin flip ---
so the negative extends rather than reverses.

The useful positive finding is independent of the mechanism under test: \textbf{raw elliptical
corrections are unrecoverable, scoring $0.0000$ recall at $k=5$ across all ten seeds} --- not low,
never. In a separate check, their top-1 rank is likewise indistinguishable from chance. A memory
system that archives conversational turns without resolving coreference loses \textbf{100\% of
corrections}, and loses them silently --- the index always returns something, just never the right
thing.

\vspace{0.6em}
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} conversational memory, retrieval-augmented generation, knowledge updates,
embedding geometry, pre-registration, negative results, coreference resolution
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

The goal that motivates this work can be stated in one sentence: \emph{that a language model never
forgets what I told it}. The emphasis is on \emph{told}. Retaining what a model \emph{read} ---
long documents, large contexts, a knowledge base --- is a crowded field with enormous resources
behind it. Retaining what a specific person \emph{said}, across sessions, including the case where
that person later \emph{corrects} it, is a structurally different problem: it persists beyond the
forward pass, it belongs to one person rather than a corpus, and it has versions. A corpus does not
contradict itself; a conversation does, constantly.

The canonical task of that goal is a versioned fact: \emph{``the director of X is Ana''}, later
\emph{``no, it's Beto''}, then \emph{``who directs X?''}. This is precisely what the
\textbf{knowledge updates} category of LongMemEval~\cite{longmemeval} measures at the system level.
Our contribution is not the task but the \emph{instrument}: we isolate the \textbf{indexing
mechanism} with everything else held fixed, which a full-system benchmark cannot do.

The mechanism under test is what we call \textbf{budding} (\emph{gemación}): when a memory is
revised, do not overwrite it --- deposit the new version in a \emph{nearby} direction, so that
proximity itself encodes the correlation between versions. Its lineage is honourable: Kanerva's
sparse distributed memory writes to neighbouring addresses and reads the neighbourhood, and the
Differentiable Neural Computer solves temporal linking with an $N \times N$ matrix, which is exactly
why it did not scale. The contribution of budding, if it worked, would be that \textbf{geometry
replaces the link matrix} --- $O(1)$ per write instead of $O(N^2)$.

It does not work. This paper reports why, in three pre-registered experiments, and reports the one
finding that survived and is useful to practitioners.

\subsection{Contributions}

\begin{enumerate}
\item A pre-registered negative for geometric indexing of versioned memory, replicated across
      \textbf{three regimes} --- fixed-step displacement, bounded displacement, and elliptical
      corrections --- with a distinct mechanism identified in each (Sections~\ref{sec:e1},
      \ref{sec:e2}, \ref{sec:e3}).
\item An \textbf{adversarial test of our own conclusion}: the third regime was designed specifically
      to favour the mechanism, by removing the property that explained its failure in the second.
      It still lost.
\item A quantitative crossover point: geometric anchoring only outperforms coreference hydration
      when the hydrator fails on $\tau^{*} \approx 0.45$ of corrections or more.
\item An independent, actionable finding: \textbf{raw elliptical corrections are never retrieved}
      ($0.0000$ recall at $k=5$, 10/10 seeds; top-1 at chance in a separate check), so archiving
      conversational turns without coreference resolution loses every correction, silently.
\item Two methodological results: a four-part \textbf{encoder admission gate} that would have saved
      two lost nights, and a case study in \textbf{a mean concealing its distribution} --- the third
      such case in this research programme.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related work}

\paragraph{Benchmarks.} LongMemEval~\cite{longmemeval} defines five abilities for long-term
conversational memory, of which \emph{knowledge updates} --- a fact stated in one session is changed
in a later one --- is precisely our task. It measures complete systems (LLM + memory + prompt), where
the source of an effect cannot be isolated. We deliberately work one level below.
We therefore do not claim the task as novel: it is a controlled, synthetic instance of a category
that already has a benchmark, and our contribution is the isolation of one component within it.

\paragraph{Conflict resolution in deployed systems.} Graphiti/Zep invalidates a superseded fact while
retaining the historical record with a validity window. REMem~\cite{remem} builds a hybrid memory
graph with gists and timestamped facts. NeuSymMS~\cite{neusymms} curates a neuro-symbolic memory for
persistent agents, and Supersede~\cite{supersede} diagnoses and trains for the specific
\emph{memory-update gap}. Mem0 and Letta offer memory as a service. All of them operate in the
\emph{application layer}: graphs, metadata, explicit pointers, symbolic invalidation. None places the
index \emph{inside} the network.

\paragraph{Deterministic freshness.} A recent line of work~\cite{freshness} argues that resolving
memory conflicts should not be delegated to the model's judgement but to a deterministic rule. Our
own geometric measurements converge on the same design verdict from a different direction ---
\emph{geometry to cluster, metadata to order} --- and our negative results are therefore an
independent, quantified confirmation of that position rather than a competitor to it.

\paragraph{Lineage of the mechanism under test.} Budding descends from Kanerva's sparse distributed
memory~\cite{kanerva}, which writes to neighbouring addresses and reads the neighbourhood, and whose
read operation is formally close to softmax attention~\cite{sdmattention}. The Differentiable Neural
Computer~\cite{dnc} solves temporal linking with an explicit $N \times N$ link matrix, which is
precisely the cost that budding aims to avoid by letting geometry carry the relation instead.

\paragraph{Retrieval indices inside the network.} Memorizing Transformers~\cite{memorizing} attach a
$k$NN memory that is not differentiable; Memory Layers~\cite{memlayers} train the index but with
parametric values rather than retrieved content; HOLA~\cite{hola} and Hybrid Associative
Memories~\cite{ham} combine a recurrent state with an exact bounded cache. All of these live
\emph{within} a sequence: their memory is born and dies in the forward pass. Persistence across
sessions --- our setting --- is left to the application layer, and the two obstacles to closing that
gap are that gradients do not flow through top-$k$ selection and that a frozen index goes stale as
the encoder moves~\cite{basu,querydrift}.

\paragraph{Reported numbers are not comparable.} On LongMemEval with GPT-4o, one report gives Zep
$63.8\%$ against Mem0 $49.0\%$, while Mem0 reports $94.4\%$ under a different configuration. The
discrepancy is methodological, not a difference in capability. This is exactly the situation that
motivates measuring a single mechanism on a controlled bench.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Task}

$N = 3000$ synthetic entities, each with one attribute drawn from five templates
(\emph{director}, \emph{headquarters}, \emph{founder}, \emph{chief engineer},
\emph{main supplier}), a value drawn from that attribute's pool, and a natural-language query
(\emph{``Who directs Helios Laboratory?''}). A revision assigns a new value to the same entity. The
index stores directions; a query retrieves the top-$k$ ($k=5$) by cosine similarity.

Three metrics, all computed per seed and aggregated with Student's $t$ 95\% confidence intervals on
9 degrees of freedom (10 seeds $\times$ 1000 sub-sampled entities):

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{CURRENT} --- the entry holding the latest revision is in the top-$k$.
\item \textbf{PREVIOUS} --- the entry holding the preceding revision is in the top-$k$.
\item \textbf{COVERAGE} --- both are, simultaneously.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Conditions}

\begin{itemize}
\item \texttt{none} --- no archive. Floor control.
\item \texttt{overwrite} --- the revision replaces the original. The old version is lost by
      construction; its PREVIOUS score must be $0$, which is our task-validity control.
\item \texttt{duplicates} --- both versions stored at the true embedding of their own text. This is
      the baseline budding must beat.
\item \texttt{budding} --- the revision's \emph{content} stored at a direction \emph{anchored} to the
      original: $\hat{v} = \mathrm{normalize}(E_0 + \varepsilon\, t)$, with $t$ a random unit tangent
      to $E_0$ and $\varepsilon = 0.30$ inherited from earlier geometric measurements and never
      re-tuned.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Pre-registration protocol}

Every prediction, threshold, and falsification clause in this paper was written and \textbf{frozen
with a SHA-256 hash before the corresponding data existed}. Hashes are recorded in
\texttt{PREREG\_HASH.txt} with timestamps in the repository's commit history. Three properties of the
protocol are worth stating explicitly, because they are what make the negatives interpretable:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Effective margin fixed in advance.} A difference counts only if it exceeds $0.02$
      absolute \emph{and} its CI does not cross zero.
\item \textbf{Blocking controls run first.} Each experiment declares a control whose failure voids
      the rest. They are evaluated before the primary prediction, and the analysis script aborts if
      one fails.
\item \textbf{Falsification committed in advance.} Each pre-registration states what conclusion
      follows if the primary prediction fails, \emph{including} the commitment to close the line
      rather than try another variant.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Encoder admission gate}
\label{sec:gate}

Before any data is generated, the encoder must pass four checks. This gate exists because its absence
cost two nights of work on a misdiagnosed failure, and one line of it would have caught the problem
immediately:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{C1 --- unique-vector census.} How many distinct vectors does the corpus produce?
\item \textbf{C2 --- value discrimination.} Do two versions of the same fact differ?
\item \textbf{C3 --- entity discrimination.} Do two different entities differ?
\item \textbf{C4 --- identification by top-1 and rank, never by AUC.}
\end{enumerate}

C4 is not pedantry. In one failed attempt an AUC of $0.97$ coexisted with a top-1 of $0.13$: the
score distribution separated well on average while the correct item was almost never ranked first.
AUC is the wrong instrument for a retrieval gate.

\paragraph{A concrete encoder failure.} \texttt{nomic-embed-text} served through Ollama collapses
\emph{every capitalised token to a single vector}. Measured cosine between unrelated proper nouns:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lcc}
\toprule
pair & capitalised & lower-cased \\
\midrule
Helios / Vantor & $\mathbf{1.000000}$ (bit-identical) & $0.407$ \\
Ana / Beto      & $\mathbf{1.000000}$                 & $0.349$ \\
cat / dog (control) & $0.603$                         & $0.603$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

This is not an endpoint artefact (reproduced on \texttt{/api/embeddings} and \texttt{/api/embed},
singly and batched) nor machine-specific. It explains an earlier corpus that yielded only 75 unique
vectors out of 3000 --- exactly $5$ attributes $\times$ $15$ numeric suffixes, the only non-capitalised
things that varied. \textbf{The fix is to lower-case the text before encoding}, after which the gate
opens: 400/400 unique vectors, top-1 $0.975$, median rank $0$. All experiments below use lower-cased
text.

\section{Experiment 1: fixed-step budding}
\label{sec:e1}

\subsection{Single revision}

With one revision per entity, all archive conditions sit at the ceiling:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
condition & CURRENT & PREVIOUS & COVERAGE \\
\midrule
\texttt{none}       & $0.0000$ & $0.0000$ & $0.0000$ \\
\texttt{overwrite}  & $1.0000$ & $0.0000$ & $0.0000$ \\
\texttt{duplicates} & $0.9992$ & $0.9988$ & $0.9988$ \\
\texttt{budding}    & $0.9928$ & $0.9928$ & $0.9928$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\textbf{P1 (primary) does not confirm}: budding $-$ duplicates in COVERAGE $= -0.0060$,
95\% CI $[-0.0077, -0.0043]$, against a required $+0.02$.

\textbf{P2 (control) passes}: \texttt{overwrite} scores exactly $0.0000$ on PREVIOUS, so the task
measures what it claims. \textbf{P3 passes}: anchoring costs nothing on retrieving the current value
($-0.0072$, floor $-0.02$).

We stress the correct reading: with both conditions above $0.99$, this is a \textbf{negative without
power}, not a demonstration of equivalence. The ceiling is what motivated Experiment~1b.

\subsection{Scaling the number of revisions}

Populating the cluster forces the geometry to work. COVERAGE as a function of $K$ revisions:

\midrule

2 & $0.9914$ & $0.0567$ & $\mathbf{-0.9347}$ $[-0.9390, -0.9304]$ \\
4 & $0.9482$ & $0.0000$ & $\mathbf{-0.9482}$ $[-0.9537, -0.9427]$ \\
8 & $0.2822$ & $0.0000$ & $\mathbf{-0.2822}$ $[-0.2912, -0.2732]$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\textbf{The mechanism, measured.} Cosine of the current entry against the query, by revision index:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lcc}
\toprule
$r$ & \texttt{budding} (fixed step) & \texttt{duplicates} (true text) \\
\midrule
0 & $0.8106$ & --- \\
1 & $0.7777$ & $0.7887$ \\
2 & $0.6792$ & $0.7683$ \\
4 & $\mathbf{0.3235}$ & $0.8064$ \\
6 & $\mathbf{-0.1391}$ & $0.8187$ \\
8 & $\mathbf{-0.1391}$ & $0.8015$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

With a fixed step $\varepsilon$ per revision the cluster performs an \textbf{unbounded random walk}:
the arc grows without limit ($\approx 0.30$ rad per step; $2.36$ rad at $r=8$), so from $r \approx 4$
the current entry is farther from the query than an arbitrary distractor and stops entering the
top-$k$. Under \texttt{duplicates} each revision is the real embedding of its own text and stays at
$\approx 0.80$ for all $r$: there is no drift because there is no induced displacement.

This is not an artefact --- it is a property of budding \emph{as specified}, with $\varepsilon$ fixed
and explicitly non-re-tunable by the pre-registration.

\section{Experiment 2: bounded budding}
\label{sec:e2}

If unbounded drift is the problem, bounding it should suffice. We pre-registered a variant,
\texttt{orbit}, in which every revision is placed at distance $\varepsilon$ from the
\emph{original anchor} rather than from the previous revision, so the displacement cannot accumulate.
A decaying-step variant and the original fixed-step condition were included as controls.

\textbf{The repair works exactly as designed.} P-A4 (mechanistic): cosine slope per revision is
$+0.0000$ for \texttt{orbit} against $-0.1410$ for the fixed step; \texttt{orbit} sits flat at
$0.8177$ for every $K$. The blocking control P-A3 also passes: the fixed step reproduces its collapse
($0.0000$ at $K=4$), so the harness is comparable across campaigns.

\textbf{And the mechanism still loses.} P-A1 (primary): COVERAGE at $K=8$,
\texttt{orbit} $-$ \texttt{duplicates} $= \mathbf{-0.0864}$, 95\% CI $[-0.1014, -0.0714]$. It loses at
all four values of $K$.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lcccc}
\toprule
$K$ & \texttt{orbit} & \texttt{decay} & \texttt{fixed} & \texttt{duplicates} \\
\midrule
1 & $0.9946$ & $0.9946$ & $0.9946$ & $0.9991$ \\
2 & $0.9747$ & $0.6805$ & $0.0546$ & $0.9914$ \\
4 & $0.8628$ & $0.0282$ & $0.0000$ & $0.9482$ \\
8 & $0.1958$ & $0.0000$ & $0.0000$ & $0.2822$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\textbf{Why.} The cosine table gives it in one line: \texttt{duplicates} sits at $0.8540$,
\texttt{orbit} at $0.8177$. Both are flat. The difference is a \textbf{constant toll of
$\approx 0.036$} paid for displacing the entry $\varepsilon$ away from the anchor, and that toll is
enough to lose the top-$k$ competition against entries belonging to other entities.

The underlying reason is that $\mathrm{emb}(v_r)$ --- the embedding of that version's real text ---
\textbf{is already optimally placed}, because it contains the entity the query mentions. Any
artificial displacement, however bounded, can only move it away. \emph{The geometry had nothing to
add: the encoder had already put each version where it belonged.}

Per the falsification clause committed in advance, budding was closed as an indexing mechanism
without trying a third geometry.

\section{Experiment 3: elliptical corrections}
\label{sec:e3}

\subsection{The objection to our own conclusion}

The closing argument of Section~\ref{sec:e2} rests on a property of the \emph{generator}, not of
corrections. Our generator builds every revision as a fully-specified template, entity included.
Real conversational corrections are \textbf{elliptical}: \emph{``no, it's Beto''}, \emph{``sorry, I
meant Beto''}. They do not name the entity, because the interlocutor already knows it.

In that regime the argument inverts: if the text does not carry the retrieval key, the encoder
\emph{cannot} place it near the query, and geometry would be the only available source of that key.
So we tested our own conclusion where it should fail.

\paragraph{Viability check.} Cosine against the query, $N=3000$: self-contained revision $0.8541$,
elliptical revision $0.4247$. An elliptical correction belonging to a \emph{different} entity scores
$0.4064$ --- statistically indistinguishable from the right one. In a smaller pilot ($N=60$) the
top-1 rank of the correct elliptical correction was $0.0167$, i.e.\ exactly the chance rate $1/60$.

\subsection{The honest baseline}

In this regime the rival is \textbf{not} bare \texttt{duplicates}, which we already know fails. It is
\texttt{duplicates} + \textbf{coreference hydration}: rewriting \emph{``no, it's Beto''} into
\emph{``The director of Helios is Beto''} \emph{before} archiving, which is what deployed systems do
and which they can do cheaply, since the conversation is at hand at write time.

We therefore swept the hydrator's failure rate $\tau$: each entry is stored hydrated with probability
$1-\tau$ and raw with probability $\tau$. Note that $\tau = 0$ reproduces the regime of
Section~\ref{sec:e2} exactly, which gives us a blocking comparability control.

\subsection{Results}

CURRENT at $K=8$:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lccccc}
\toprule
$\tau$ & $0.00$ & $0.05$ & $0.10$ & $0.40$ & $1.00$ \\
\midrule
\texttt{hydrated}$_\tau$ & $0.5414$ & $0.5368$ & $0.5312$ & $0.4884$ & $\mathbf{0.0000}$ \\
\texttt{orbit} (flat)    & \multicolumn{5}{c}{$0.4723$} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\textbf{P-E0 (blocking) passes}: at $\tau=0$, hydration beats orbit by $+0.0691$
$[+0.0587, +0.0795]$, reproducing the toll measured in Section~\ref{sec:e2}.

\textbf{P-E2 (regime control) passes}, and is the paper's most useful number: raw elliptical
corrections score $\mathbf{0.0000}$, with a CI of exactly $[0.0000, 0.0000]$ across all ten seeds.
Not \emph{low} --- \emph{never}.

\textbf{P-E3 (mechanistic) passes}: the cosine of the stored entry falls linearly in $\tau$ with
slope $-0.4291$ (predicted $-0.426$), while \texttt{orbit} is flat at $0.8177$.

\textbf{P-E1 (primary) does not confirm.} We required a crossover at $\tau^{*} \leq 0.25$. A finer
grid, run after the verdict and reported as exploratory, locates it between $\tau = 0.40$
($-0.0161$) and $\tau = 0.50$ ($+0.0349$): $\tau^{*} \approx \mathbf{0.45}$.

\paragraph{Practical statement.} Geometric anchoring is worth its toll only if your coreference
resolver fails on \textbf{45\% or more} of corrections --- worse than a coin flip. No deployed system
is in that regime. The negative therefore \emph{extends} to the regime designed to favour the
mechanism, rather than being confined to self-contained text.

\subsection{The finding that survives}

Independently of budding: \textbf{a memory system that archives conversational turns without
resolving coreference loses 100\% of corrections.} The failure is total ($0.0000$, 10/10 seeds) and
\textbf{silent}: the index always returns five candidates, ranked confidently, none of them right.
There is no error signal, no empty result, no low-confidence flag --- the correct correction sits at
$0.4237$ and a stranger's at $0.4064$.

This is measurable, cheap to check, and applies to every system in the application-layer table of
Section~2.

\paragraph{Structural note.} An elliptical correction carries no entity information by construction,
so the space of possible texts is tiny: four phrasings $\times$ 60 distinct values $=$ \textbf{240
possible strings} for 24{,}000 stored entries. Two different entities corrected to the same value
produce \emph{bit-identical} vectors. That degeneracy is not an artefact of our design --- it is the
arithmetic form of the phenomenon under study, and it is why exact ties had to be broken explicitly
in the ranking code rather than assumed away.

\section{A methodological result: means conceal distributions}

The pre-registration recorded a \emph{point} prediction, $\tau^{*} \approx 0.075$, derived from a
linear model of the cosine curves. The cosine mechanism behaved exactly as predicted --- P-E3 passes,
slope $-0.4291$ against $-0.426$ predicted --- and the crossover \emph{still} landed at $0.45$, six
times later.

The reason is worth stating because it generalises. \texttt{hydrated}$_\tau$ does not degrade
uniformly: each entry is either \textbf{intact} ($0.854$) or \textbf{ruined} ($0.42$), never in
between. The \emph{mean} crosses \texttt{orbit} at $\tau \approx 0.08$, but top-$k$ is decided
entry by entry, and at $\tau = 0.10$ ninety per cent of queries still have a perfectly placed entry.
Orbit, by contrast, pays its toll on \emph{every} entry.

A second-order effect pushes the same way: as competing entries degrade, they stop competing for the
top-$k$, which \emph{helps} the healthy entry. This is why \texttt{hydrated}$_{0.40}$ scores $0.4884$
rather than the $0.6 \times 0.5414 = 0.325$ that a multiplicative model predicts.

\textbf{Rule of thumb:} when a prediction is derived from an average, check the shape of the
distribution before trusting the number. This is the third instance of a mean concealing its
distribution in this research programme.

\section{Limitations}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Non-parametric index over a frozen encoder.} Nothing here speaks to an index
      \textbf{co-trained inside the network}, where directions would be learned representations
      rather than fixed text embeddings, and where ``the encoder already placed it correctly'' stops
      applying by construction. That remains the open problem, with its two known obstacles:
      gradients do not flow through top-$k$ selection, and the index goes stale as the encoder moves.
\item \textbf{Synthetic corpus.} Entities, attributes, and the four elliptical phrasings are
      synthetic and fixed; they are not a sample of human corrections.
\item \textbf{Hydration is modelled as all-or-nothing.} A real resolver can fail in worse ways ---
      hydrating with the \emph{wrong} entity --- which we do not model and which would be more
      damaging than not hydrating at all.
\item \textbf{$\tau$ is imposed, not measured.} The experiment yields the crossover point; it does
      not tell you where a given production system sits on that axis.
\item \textbf{Single encoder family.} All results use \texttt{nomic-embed-text}; the capitalisation
      pathology of Section~\ref{sec:gate} is a reminder that encoder-specific behaviour is real.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}

We set out to test whether the geometry of an embedding index can carry the relation between
versions of a remembered fact. Across three pre-registered experiments the answer is no, each time
for an identifiable reason: with a fixed step the cluster walks away; bounded, it pays a constant
toll; and in the elliptical regime designed to favour it, it only wins when the alternative is
already broken beyond usefulness.

The negative is strengthened, not weakened, by the third experiment: we built the condition under
which our own explanation should fail, and it did not. What remains is a design verdict that agrees
with the deterministic-freshness position from an independent direction --- \emph{geometry to
cluster, metadata to order} --- and one practical warning with a number attached: if your memory
system stores conversational turns as written, every correction that does not name its subject is
already lost, and nothing in the system will tell you.
